## 1. How many people work in Nigerian BPO/contact centres, who are the largest employers, and what government or industry targets exist?

### Takeaway
There is no current, authoritative headcount for Nigerian BPO/contact centres: the only sourced count is 16,540 workers and about $286m revenue from a 2021 Knowledge Executive survey, while 2023–2025 revenue estimates range from about $500m to $1.14bn depending on definition. Government targets (50,000 BPO jobs via NATEP/Lab Four, 1 million direct export jobs via NATEP, 5 million via NITDA's outsourcing strategy) are orders of magnitude above any measured headcount and none has a published progress report.

### Cited Findings

This is the most recent sourced headcount found and is five years old — [Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/technology/nigerias-outsourcing-sub-sector-worth-285-8m-employs-16540/); [PressReader copy dated 8 Sep 2021](https://www.pressreader.com/nigeria/the-guardian-nigeria/20210908/281827171883167)
- Same 2021 report: Africa's combined domestic and international GBS sector worth $15.1bn, forecast to reach $19.8bn by 2023; survey covered 500+ providers in 19 countries; GBS workforces ranged from 1,200 (Mozambique) to 261,082 (South Africa) — [Ventureburn, Sep 2021](https://ventureburn.com/2021/09/african-global-business-services-sector-to-grow-to-19-8-billion-by-2023-research/)
- Nigeria's "outsourcing market" revenue: about $980m in 2023, projected to exceed $1.8bn by 2028 (attributed to SeamlessHR/AOPN commentary) — [TechEconomy AOPN tag page](https://techeconomy.ng/tag/aopn/). This is a proposal, not a measured outcome — [ODI](https://odi.org/en/publications/developing-a-digital-trade-strategy-in-nigeria/)

### Inferences
- A defensible range for formal Nigerian BPO/contact-centre employment in 2026 is "low tens of thousands": 16,540 in 2021, plus firm-level points (Tek Experts about 2,000; Outsource Global 750–6,000; iSON's Nigerian share of a 15,000–18,000 pan-African workforce). Any single 2026 number would be an extrapolation, not a finding.
- The revenue estimates ($286m in 2021; $500m+ to $1.14bn in 2023–2025) are not one series: the higher figures include staff outsourcing/HR outsourcing, which in Nigeria is a large business (see section 5 on bank contract staff).
- The gap between targets (50,000 to 5 million jobs) and measured headcount (about 16,500) means the outsourced BPO segment alone is a small buyer base for a per-candidate product today; in-house contact centres at telcos, banks and fintechs, and outsourced bank/telco contract staff, are likely the larger near-term pool.
- AI exposure of entry-level BPO work (68% of the workforce; 40% of tasks affected by 2030) is a demand risk for volume-based pricing over a five-year horizon.

### Gaps
- No headcount found for Concentrix, CCI Global, Teleperformance/Majorel, or ipNX in Nigeria, or for telco in-house contact centres (MTN, Airtel, Glo); searches returned nothing usable.
- No customer-support headcount for OPay, PalmPay, Moniepoint or Kuda.
- No 2022–2026 Nigerian BPO headcount from Knowledge Executive, Everest Group, Genesis Analytics or a Nigerian association (AOPN). The Caribou/Genesis 2025 report and Everest's August 2024 Africa report probably contain country tables but could not be opened.
- No published progress report against the NOS, NATEP or OTNI job targets.

## 2. South Africa, Kenya, Egypt and Ghana: headcount, growth, export revenue and attrition

### Takeaway
South Africa and Egypt are the only two African markets at six-figure scale (South Africa roughly 150,000–270,000 depending on whether domestic work is counted; Egypt 240,000–300,000+ by ITIDA-linked counts), while Kenya sits at about 35,000–40,000 and Ghana at about 20,000. South Africa added a record 26,346 net international jobs in 2025, 84% of recent new hires being frontline voice agents, but its 500,000-job 2030 target is now considered out of reach.

**South Africa**

### Inferences

- South African gross hiring is far above the 26,346 net figure: at 30–40% attrition on 150,000–260,000 agents, replacement hiring alone is roughly 45,000–100,000 a year, giving roughly 70,000–130,000 gross frontline hires a year. This is arithmetic on cited ranges, not a published statistic.
- Kenya's and Ghana's targets (100,000 new jobs each) are 2.5 to 5 times current headcount; planning should use current headcount, not targets.
- Egypt's job numbers mix cumulative "jobs created" with stock headcount; 300,000+ may be cumulative.

## 3. Contact-centre attrition, time-to-proficiency, cost per hire, training cost and cost of early attrition

### Takeaway
Global benchmarks put contact-centre agent attrition at roughly 30–45% a year (NICE: 39% in 2024, down from 49% in 2023), with full replacement cost commonly quoted at $10,000–$20,000 per agent in the US; the only Africa-specific cost figures are South African vendor estimates of R18,000–R60,000 per failed hire. Early attrition is where the cost concentrates: an ICMI figure (seen only second-hand) says 65–70% of first-year attrition happens in the first 90 days.

### Cited Findings
**Attrition rates (global)**
- NICE measured 39% average contact-centre agent attrition in 2024, down from 49% in 2023 — [NICE](https://www.nice.com/answers/cost-of-high-agent-turnover-in-contact-centers)
- Average call-centre turnover of 30–40% a year, with some centres at 80–100% — [Time Doctor](https://www.timedoctor.com/blog/call-center-turnover-rate/)
- ContactBabel's 2023 UK figure was 26% voluntary agent attrition; US surveys commonly give 30–45%; McKinsey is cited for up to 60% in some contact centres (all as relayed by trade sources) — [Call Centre Helper](https://callcentrehelper.com/attrition-bpo-employee-turnover-229379.htm)
- Under 30% a year is treated as healthy for most BPO operations — [Outsource Accelerator glossary](https://www.outsourceaccelerator.com/glossary/call-center-attrition/)
- 2025 turnover of about 38–45% attributed to ICMI and Deloitte **[unverified; aggregator attribution not confirmed]** — [StealthAgents](https://stealthagents.com/research/bpo-agent-attrition-statistics-2026)

**Time to proficiency and training**
- McKinsey: new hires need about four to six months to reach peak proficiency — [McKinsey operations blog](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/operations-blog/rewriting-the-playbook-gen-ais-impact-on-learning-in-contact-centers)
- 55% of contact centres spend 6–12 weeks training and onboarding new agents (ProcedureFlow survey) — [ProcedureFlow report](https://procedureflow.com/reports/state-of-contact-center-training-report.pdf)
- Vendor ranges: 4–8 weeks to proficiency for most centres, up to 6–9 months for some; ramp of 60–90 days — [ScreenSteps](https://blog.screensteps.com/call-center-time-to-proficiency); [Vonage](https://www.vonage.com/resources/articles/call-center-agent-attrition/)
- A McKinsey exhibit lists $4,000–$7,000 upfront training cost per agent; its underlying source is a 2000 Purdue benchmarking study, so it is dated — [McKinsey, "Customer care: the future talent factory"](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Business%20Functions/Operations/Our%20Insights/Customer%20care%20The%20future%20talent%20factory/Customer-care-The-future-talent-factory.pdf)

**Cost per hire and cost of attrition (global/US)**
- SHRM 2025 US benchmark: $1,200 median and $5,475 average cost per non-executive hire (recruiting cost only; excludes training and ramp) (page attribution uncertain; relayed by a secondary source) — [StealthAgents replacement-cost page](https://stealthagents.com/research/bpo-employee-attrition-replacement-cost-statistics-2026)
- Full cost of replacing a frontline agent: $10,000–$20,000+, including recruiting, training and lost productivity; one breakdown gives training $1,000–$2,000 and ramp-up productivity loss $6,750–$13,317 — [Vonage](https://www.vonage.com/resources/articles/call-center-agent-attrition/); [StealthAgents](https://stealthagents.com/research/bpo-agent-attrition-statistics-2026)

### Inferences
- The South African replacement-cost range (R18,000–R60,000) is roughly two to six months of an agent's pay (R8,000–R12,000 a month), consistent with the Gallup 0.5x–2x-salary rule; a salary-multiple method is the most defensible way to estimate cost of a bad hire in Nigeria, Kenya or Ghana, where no local figure exists.
- If 65–70% of first-year exits happen within 90 days, most attrition cost is incurred before the agent reaches proficiency (6–12 weeks training plus 4–6 months to peak), which is the window a pre-hire behavioural assessment claims to address. The supporting statistic is second-hand and should be presented as indicative.
- A 1,000-seat African operation at 30–40% attrition makes 300–400 replacement hires a year; at South African vendor cost estimates that is roughly R5m–R24m a year in replacement cost. This is illustrative arithmetic.

- SQM, ContactBabel 2025/2026 and Deloitte GBS survey figures were not retrieved directly.

## 4. Nigerian agent banking: agents, POS terminals, top networks, churn, fraud, and the CBN October 2025 guidelines and exclusivity rule in 2026

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- The agent base (1.4–2 million) is about 100 times the measured Nigerian BPO workforce (16,540), so agent networks are by far the largest "frontline" population reachable through a handful of buyers (OPay, Moniepoint, PalmPay, FirstBank, GTBank).
- Exclusivity changes the economics of agent selection: a principal can no longer share an agent with rivals, each agent's conduct and KYC is attributable to one institution, and regulators and legislators are focused on "unprofiled agents" as a fraud vector. That plausibly raises willingness to screen agents at onboarding, but no source found shows principals actually spending on behavioural or integrity screening of agents.
- Onboarding volume cannot be measured from public data. If AMMBAN's 40–50% exit claims were even half right, and networks keep replacing agents, annual agent onboarding events would be in the hundreds of thousands; this is speculative and rests on advocacy numbers.
- Rising transaction value (+62.5% in H1 2026) alongside claimed agent exits suggests consolidation toward fewer, larger agents rather than a shrinking channel.
- Agents are independent business owners, not employees; the buyer is the principal's agent-network or risk team, and price tolerance per agent is likely far lower than per employee hire.

### Gaps
- No NIBSS, CBN or SANEF agent count dated after April 2026; no measured data on agents who exited, switched principal or were deactivated after exclusivity took effect.
- No NIBSS breakdown of fraud by POS/agent channel for 2025 or 2026; the 2025 figures cover all electronic channels.
- No per-agent onboarding or KYC cost figure for any network.
- PalmPay agent count not found. The exact per-network agent counts are December 2022 data.
- The August 2026 geo-fencing compromise and the 31 December 2026 deadlines rest on single search summaries and need checking against CBN circulars.

## 7. Nigerian unemployment and youth labour-market context that drives applicant volumes

### Takeaway
Official unemployment is low on paper (4.3% in Q2 2024 under the NBS's revised methodology, still the latest published figure found), yet large recruitment drives show extreme oversubscription: 573,680 applications for 3,927 Customs vacancies (0.7% hired) and 1.91 million applications for about 30,000 paramilitary posts. These ratios of roughly 64 to 149 applications per vacancy are the clearest evidence of screening load.

### Inferences
- Implied applications per vacancy: Customs about 146–149; CDCFIB about 64; NIS about 36; Oyo about 17; NBS 2015 about 70. A working range of 50–150 applications per entry-level vacancy for well-known employers is supported by public-sector evidence; private-sector ratios are not documented.
- The headline unemployment rate (4.3%) understates screening load because the revised NBS method counts anyone working one hour a week as employed; underemployment and informality, not open unemployment, drive mass applications. The NEET rate and the recruitment-drive data are the better indicators.
- At 50–150 applicants per hire, any per-candidate price is multiplied 50–150 times per hire, so employers will push assessment late in the funnel or demand very low unit prices; a product priced per candidate needs either a cheap top-of-funnel tier or per-hire-equivalent pricing.

- The NNPC figure of 45,689 applicants seen in one result had no vacancy count or date and is not used.

## 8. What employers currently spend per candidate or per hire on screening in these markets

### Takeaway
Almost no public per-candidate price exists for Nigeria: the main visible price is Jobberman's flat per-role assessment fee (N54,000 or N150,000 depending on the page) covering all applicants to one listing, which works out to very little per candidate at Nigerian applicant volumes. Elsewhere the only data are South African vendor estimates (recruitment cost R2,500–R4,000 per hire; agency fees 15–20% of annual salary) and the US SHRM cost-per-hire benchmark.

### Cited Findings
- Jobberman Skills Assessments: one page states N150,000 per assessment (one assessment per listing); another states an employer can test all applicants to a role for N54,000. Pricing is per test/role, not per applicant. Pages are undated, so the two prices may be from different years — [Jobberman skills assessments](https://jobberman.com/skills-assessments); [Jobberman discover page](https://jobberman.com/discover/jobberman-skills-assessments)
- Jobberman 2024 rate card: Basic job listing N25,000; other tiers N80,000 and N100,000 (Job Boost); skills assessment shown as bundled/free with a paid tier — [Jobberman Nigeria rate card 2024](https://static.jobberman.com/jobberman-nigeria-product-rate-card-2024.pdf)
- Proten International's PAss candidate-assessment platform (launched July 2022) uses a pay-as-you-use model; no naira price published — [Nairametrics, 20 Jul 2022](https://nairametrics.com/2022/07/20/proten-international-launches-candidate-assessment-platform-pass)
- Harrison Assessments (GHF Nigeria) offers pay-per-assessment or monthly/annual unlimited plans; amounts not shown — [GHF/Harrison usage options](https://ghf-psychometrics.harrisonassessments.com/usage-options.html)
- Candidate-paid screening: N7,500 drug test per paramilitary applicant (2025) — [Leadership](https://leadership.ng/ndlea-says-interior-ministry-job-seekers-exempted-from-%e2%82%a67500-drug-test-fee/)
- South Africa: recruitment cost alone R2,500–R4,000 per call-centre hire; agency fees 15–20% of annual salary (R14,400–R28,800) **[vendor estimates, unverified]** — [ShiftMate (Pietermaritzburg)](https://shiftmate.co.za/resources/blog/why-pietermaritzburg-bpo-companies-lose-58-of-new-hires-in-90-days-and-how-trial-to-hire-fixes-the-m); [ShiftMate (pre-screening)](https://shiftmate.co.za/resources/blog/how-to-reduce-call-centre-agent-turnover-with-better-pre)
- US reference: SHRM 2025 median cost per non-executive hire $1,200, average $5,475 (page attribution uncertain) — [StealthAgents](https://stealthagents.com/research/bpo-employee-attrition-replacement-cost-statistics-2026)
- International tool reference: EmployTest pre-employment tests start at $199, sold per test rather than annual licence — [Capterra South Africa](https://www.capterra.co.za/software/185208/employtest)

### Inferences
- With Jobberman's flat N54,000–N150,000 per role and 50–150+ applicants per vacancy (section 7), the incumbent effective price is on the order of N360–N3,000 per applicant for a single-vacancy role, and far lower for bulk roles with thousands of applicants. A per-candidate behavioural assessment would be competing against an anchor that is close to free per head.
- The N7,500 candidate-paid drug test shows that a single-purpose screening step is priced in the low thousands of naira per candidate in Nigeria, and that charging candidates is politically sensitive.
- In South Africa, a screening price in the low hundreds of rand per candidate would be small against a R2,500–R4,000 recruitment cost and an R18,000–R60,000 failed-hire cost, so the willingness-to-pay case is stronger there than in Nigeria on current evidence.

### Gaps
- No published per-candidate price from Nigerian assessment providers (Dragnet, Workforce Group, Proten, GHF, Phillips Consulting) or from global vendors' Africa pricing was found; this needs vendor quotes or buyer interviews.
- No data on what Nigerian BPOs, banks, fintechs or agent networks actually budget for screening per hire or per agent.
- No Kenya, Egypt or Ghana screening-cost data.
